The twenty decisions

Companion to LLM from scratch20 ideas · a diagram and a live demo for each

One question,
twenty decisions

Most AI engineering glossaries hand you twenty disconnected definitions. In a real system they are not a list — they are the path one question takes. So this page follows a single customer question, from the moment it is typed to the moment the reply appears, and stops wherever somebody had to make a decision.

The question we are following

Shakahar is an online store. Its help chat is answered by an AI assistant. At 09:12 a customer, already halfway through complaining about an order, types: Why is it late? Four words. No order number, no city, and “it” is doing all the work. Everything on this page is what has to happen for a correct, useful reply to arrive three seconds later.

The same question, answered twice by the same AI model. The first build takes fifteen seconds and gets it wrong. The difference is not a smarter model — it is twenty engineering choices, one per chapter of this page. No prior knowledge needed: every chapter starts with a plain-language line and a diagram.

before t=0

The work that happened last week

Two decisions are already locked in before the customer types anything. They were made when the store's help pages were loaded into the system, and nothing clever later can undo them.

01

Semantic chunking

split documents where the meaning changes, not every N tokens

A retriever can only return pieces that someone decided to create. Fixed-size splitting cuts every 400 tokens regardless of content, which routinely severs a heading from its answer. Semantic chunking cuts at topic boundaries instead, so each piece is about one thing.

In plain words Think of a cookbook. Cut it every twenty pages and you will slice a recipe in half. Cut it at the end of each recipe and every dish stays whole.

help page: “Delivery and delays” · 1,180 pieces longsemantic

where this shows up

Shakahar's help guide covers delivery, returns and payments on one long page. Split by topic, “parcel held at a closed hub — what we do” survives as one findable piece.piece 3 · topic: delayed parcels · makes sense on its own: yes

what happens without it

The every-N-pages splitter ends a piece straight after the words “the most common reason is”. The assistant is handed a sentence that promises an answer and never gives one, so it invents the rest.

02

BM25

rank by keyword overlap, weighted toward rare terms

BM25 scores a document by how often the query's words appear in it, boosting rare terms and discounting long documents that match by sheer size. It has no idea what words mean — which is precisely why it never misses an exact error code.

In plain words This is the search you already know — the one that matches the words you typed. It pays most attention to unusual words.

word-match search · 4 help pagestry: TRK-88213 · refund · delivery

where this shows up

A customer pastes a tracking number, TRK-88213. It appears on one page out of four thousand, so word-match search puts that page first. Rare words are strong signals.“TRK-88213” appears on 1 page · “order” appears on 3,900 — so the rare one decides the ranking

what happens without it

Meaning-based search reads TRK-88213 as “some tracking code” and returns a general shipping page. Exact codes, order numbers and product names are exactly where it is weakest.

t + 0 ms

Making the question searchable

“Why is it late?” is a perfectly good sentence and a useless search box query. The word carrying all the meaning — “it” — is in the previous message, not this one.

03

Query rewriting

turn a conversation turn into a standalone query

Retrieval has no memory of the conversation — it sees one string. Query rewriting folds the earlier turns back in, producing a query that stands on its own while keeping the user's original intent.

In plain words If a friend asks “why is it late?”, you know what “it” means because you heard the last two sentences. A search box did not.

where this shows up

Three messages of chat fold into one question that names the order, the city and the date — the words the help pages can actually be matched against.“Why is it late?” → “order 4471 delivery delay Atlanta 3 September”

what happens without it

The search runs on “why is it late”, matches every page containing the word late, and the one page that explains the delay never gets a chance.

t + 90 ms

Finding the evidence

Two kinds of search with opposite blind spots, run together, then a slow careful second look at what survived. The assistant can only reason about pages it is actually handed.

04

Hybrid search

fuse keyword ranking with semantic ranking

Keyword search catches exact strings and misses paraphrase. Vector search catches paraphrase and fumbles exact strings. Hybrid runs both and fuses the two ranked lists, buying recall without giving up precision on identifiers.

In plain words One search matches the words you typed. The other matches what you meant. Running both catches what either one alone would miss.

question: “order 4471 delivery delay Atlanta”hybrid

where this shows up

The page that explains this delay never uses the word “late” — it says “parcels held when a hub closes early”. Word-match ranks it 4th, meaning-match ranks it 1st. Run together, both the tracking record and the explanation reach the assistant.final score = half the word-match score + half the meaning-match score

what happens without it

Pick one kind of search and a whole class of questions quietly gets worse, while your average score still looks perfectly healthy.

05

Reranking

a slow, careful second pass over a small candidate set

The first search is optimised to be fast over millions of documents, so it is approximate. A reranker reads query and candidate together and scores the pairing properly. It is far too slow for the whole corpus and perfectly affordable for twenty candidates.

In plain words A quick sort of the whole shelf, then a careful read of the eight books you actually pulled out.

where this shows up

Only four help pages fit into the reply. Reranking moves the page that explains the hub closure from 6th to 1st, so the assistant reads the real reason instead of a general delivery page.careful score — “hub closed early” 0.91 · “delivery times overview” 0.34

what happens without it

You compensate by pasting in twenty pages instead. It costs three times as much, takes longer, and buries the useful sentence in the middle where it is most likely to be skimmed over.

t + 450 ms

Choosing the model, and what it reads

One decision about which model answers, and two about what the model is really reading — the unit your bill and your size limits are both measured in.

06

Model routing

choose the capability the request actually needs

Sending every request to your strongest model is the most expensive way to be wrong about latency. A router reads intent and cost and picks a model per request — and because the router itself makes decisions, it needs tests, fallbacks and a misroute metric like anything else.

In plain words A hospital triage desk. A sore throat does not need the surgeon.

10,000 customer questions a dayrouted
—daily cost
—p50 latency

where this shows up

“What are your store hours?” is a lookup and goes to the small, fast model in a third of a second. “Why is my order late and can I get a refund?” needs real reasoning, so it goes to the bigger one and takes two seconds.question type = complaint + refund → big model · if it times out → small model

what happens without it

Either every simple question is answered at premium prices, or every hard question gets a fast, confident, shallow reply. A wrongly routed question is the hardest failure to notice, because the answer still reads fine.

07

Tokenization

text becomes reusable pieces, then numbers

The model never sees your sentence. It sees a list of integers, produced by splitting text into pieces that are often smaller than words. Every context limit and every invoice counts tokens, not words — which is why a log line can cost four times what an English sentence of the same width costs.

In plain words The model reads in pieces, a bit like syllables. “Late” is one piece. An order number is a fistful.

where this shows up

The assistant is allowed about 8,000 pieces of reading per reply. Four pages of plain help text fit easily; four pages full of order numbers and timestamps do not — so the budget is counted in pieces, not characters.“TRK-88213” → TRK · - · 8 · 8 · 2 · 1 · 3 seven pieces for one tracking number

what happens without it

You size the prompt by counting letters, it passes testing on tidy English, and it overflows the first time a customer pastes an order confirmation email — or writes in Telugu.

08

Mixture of experts

many internal networks, a few active per token

An MoE layer holds many parallel sub-networks. A gating router sends each token to a small subset, and their weighted outputs merge into one result. The model gets large total capacity at small per-token cost — and the experts are learned statistical groupings, not tidy human departments like “SQL” or “French”.

In plain words A large consultancy where every question goes to two specialists, not the whole firm. You still pay to keep everyone on the payroll.

where this shows up

Choosing between two models: one is 32B and uses all of itself every time; the other is 120B in total but only wakes about 5B per piece of text. The second needs the memory of a big model and runs at close to the speed of a small one.held in memory: 120B · actually working on this piece: ~5B

what happens without it

You read “120B” on the model's spec sheet, budget a machine for a 120B workload, and get one of the two numbers badly wrong. Both matter, and they measure different things.

t + 470 ms

Generating without wasting the GPU

Three decisions that change no word of the reply — only how long it takes, and how many customers can be served at once.

09

KV cache

store attention keys and values instead of recomputing them

Generating token 500 requires attending to the previous 499. Without a cache the model rebuilds all of that work every step. The KV cache stores each token's keys and values once, so each new token reads history instead of recomputing it — and that stored history is what fills your GPU memory.

In plain words Rather than re-reading the whole conversation before every single word it writes, the assistant keeps notes. Notes take up desk space.

where this shows up

Customers paste long order emails into the chat. Every word kept in the conversation is desk space held per customer — and that is what decides how many customers one machine can serve at the same time.notes grow with every word kept — twice the conversation, twice the desk space

what happens without it

Without notes, each new word costs more than the last, and the final paragraph takes longer to write than the first three combined. With notes, you trade that time for memory — and memory usually runs out first.

10

Speculative decoding

a small model guesses ahead, the big model checks in bulk

Decoding is sequential: one full pass of the large model per token. A small draft model proposes several tokens at once and the large model verifies them in a single pass, keeping the prefix it agrees with. Same distribution, fewer sequential steps.

In plain words A junior drafts the next few words, the expert glances at them and says “yes, yes, yes — no, from there it's this”. Same final wording, far less of the expert's time.

The reply is 300 words of fairly predictable text: “your parcel is delayed because…”. what happens without it

Nothing breaks — you simply pay full price for every word. The catch is that on unpredictable text, like codes and random IDs, the guesses miss and it becomes slightly slower. Worth measuring rather than assuming.

11

Continuous batching

refill the batch mid-flight instead of waiting for the slowest request

GPUs are efficient on batches, but requests finish at wildly different lengths. With static batching everyone waits for the longest one. Continuous batching keeps the batch open: when a request finishes, its slot is freed and a queued request joins immediately.

In plain words A checkout counter that lets the next person step up the moment someone leaves, instead of making everyone wait for the shopper with the full trolley.

where this shows up

At 09:12 five other customers are also asking questions. One needs a yes or no, another needs a long explanation. The short one leaves early and the next person starts being served in the same instant.typical wait: 4.1 s the old way → 0.6 s this way, on exactly the same hardware

what happens without it

The machine looks busy on every dashboard while everybody queues behind the longest answer. This is usually the cheapest speed-up available, because it costs nothing in accuracy.

t + 2.5 s

Doing something about it

The assistant has a theory and needs the courier's tracking record to confirm it. The moment it reaches out to another system, this stops being an AI problem and becomes an ordinary software-reliability problem.

12

Structured outputs

constrain the response to a declared schema

Code downstream needs fields, not paragraphs. A schema constrains generation so the response parses every time. But a schema governs shape, not truth — perfectly valid JSON can carry a wrong severity, so business rules still get validated after parsing.

In plain words Instead of writing a paragraph, the assistant fills in a form: refund yes or no, amount, reason.

where this shows up

The system has to decide whether to approve a shipping refund automatically. That is a box on a form, not a sentence for other software to guess at.{ "order": "4471", "reason": "hub_delay", "refund_shipping": true, "escalate": false }

what happens without it

Someone writes code that looks for the words “issue a refund”. It works for a month, then the model writes “we'll refund the shipping” and no refund happens. The opposite mistake is trusting the tidy form so completely that nobody checks whether the box is ticked correctly.

13

Model Context Protocol

one interface for discovering and calling tools

Before MCP, every AI app wrote a bespoke adapter for every system. MCP makes tools and resources discoverable through one shared interface, so a client can talk to GitHub, a database and a docs store the same way. It standardises exposure — not permission. Auth, approvals and audit remain yours.

In plain words One standard plug instead of a different adapter for every appliance in the house.

where this shows up

The assistant needs the courier's tracking system and the store's order database. Both speak the same standard, so adding the returns system next month is a line of configuration rather than a project.every system answers the same two questions: what can you do, and please do it

what happens without it

Every app builds its own connector to every system, each with its own login quirks. And the standard plug brings its own trap: being able to reach a system is not the same as being allowed to change it. Give it a read-only key.

14

ReAct

think, act, observe, decide again

Rather than planning everything up front, the agent alternates between reasoning and acting: each observation becomes evidence for the next step, so the plan can change as facts arrive. Useful autonomy needs boundaries — tool permissions, step limits, timeouts and an explicit stop rule.

In plain words Look, then decide, then look again — the way you would actually chase a late parcel, instead of guessing the whole story up front.

where this shows up

Step one checks the tracking record and finds the parcel never left Atlanta, which is not what the assistant assumed. Step two changes plan and checks hub notices instead.check tracking → “at Atlanta hub since Wed” → check hub notices → “closed early Wed” → answer

what happens without it

Either a rigid script that answers the wrong question confidently, or — with no limit on steps — an assistant that keeps retrying a broken system for nine minutes. The loop is the point; the step limit is what makes it safe to ship.

t + 2.6 s

When the tool call goes wrong

Two decisions borrowed from ordinary software engineering. They are most of what separates a demo from something a business can rely on.

15

Circuit breaker

stop calling a dependency that is already failing

When a dependency fails repeatedly, retrying adds load to something already on fire. A breaker counts failures, opens after a threshold, fails fast or falls back during a cooldown, then lets one test request through to check recovery.

In plain words If the courier's phone line is dead, stop redialling it every ten seconds. Tell the customer what you do know, and try again in a while.

The courier's system goes down. After three failures the assistant stops calling it and answers from the help pages with an honest “live tracking is unavailable right now” — immediately, rather than after a thirty-second wait.while stopped: answer from help pages, say tracking is unavailable, fail in 20 ms not 30 s

what happens without it

Every customer question turns into three more calls to a system that is already struggling, and every customer waits the full timeout to be told nothing.

16

Checkpointing

a durable snapshot of where this run stopped

Checkpointing saves a workflow's exact execution position and the state needed to continue. It is not general memory and not a chat history — it is “step 3 finished, here is what it produced, resume from here” — which is what makes human approval and crash recovery possible without redoing work.

In plain words Saving your progress in a game. If the power cuts, you restart from the last save rather than the beginning.

The refund workflow gathers the facts, drafts a refund and then waits for a supervisor. Approval arrives eleven minutes later, possibly on a different machine. A restart during the wait throws that work away. Worse, the retry re-runs steps that already had real effects — and the second refund is the one that causes the problem.

t + 2.9 s

The text you retrieved is not on your side

One decision separates an assistant that reads a page from an assistant that takes orders from it.

17

Prompt injection

untrusted text starts issuing orders

Your instructions and a retrieved page arrive in the same working context, as the same kind of thing: text. So a sentence inside a wiki page, a ticket comment or a tool result can read as a command. A warning in the system prompt is not an access control, and treating it as one is the core mistake.

In plain words A note hidden inside a document saying “ignore your manager, do this instead” — and a system naive enough to obey it, because it cannot tell the note from the manager.

after the answer

Proving it worked

The reply went out in three seconds and read well. Three decisions determine whether anyone can tell it was actually right — and where those three seconds went.

18

Recall@k

did the needed evidence appear in the first k results?

Recall@k asks one narrow question: was the required document anywhere inside the top k. It says nothing about the answer's correctness and gives no credit for exact position — it measures coverage, which is exactly why it isolates retrieval from generation.

In plain words Did the right page even make it into the small pile the assistant was allowed to read? If it did not, no amount of clever wording will save the answer.

where this shows up

Shakahar's assistant was accused of making things up. Checking showed that in a third of test questions the correct help page never reached it at all. That is a search problem, and swapping the model would not have touched it.right page in the top 3: 58% of questions · in the top 10: 91% → fix the splitting and the ranking

what happens without it

Every failure looks like the model's fault, because the finished answer is the only thing anyone measures.

19

LLM-as-a-judge

one model grades another against a rubric

When correctness cannot be checked by string match, a second model scores the response against a rubric. It scales evaluation across thousands of outputs — and it is a biased, inconsistent instrument that favours confident, well-formatted prose. A confident score is not truth; calibrate the judge against human labels before you trust it.

In plain words Marking homework with a second student instead of a teacher. Fast and cheap, and it quietly rewards neat handwriting.

where this shows up

Before every release, 200 saved questions are replayed and graded on one checkable thing: is every statement in the reply actually present in the pages it was given? That is what keeps the marker honest.score 1–5: is each claim backed by a retrieved page? · agreement with human markers: 0.71

what happens without it

You ship on gut feel — or worse, an unchecked marker prints a confident 4.4 out of 5 every week while the fluent, wrong answers sail through.

20

Distributed tracing

one ID, one timeline, every hop

Rewriting, retrieval, reranking, generation, tools and guardrails are separate services with separate logs. A trace gives the request one shared ID and records each hop as a span with its timing and parent, so six log streams become one connected timeline — and the real bottleneck stops being a matter of opinion.

In plain words One receipt showing every stop the question made and how long each took, instead of nine separate logs that nobody can line up.

where this shows up

Customers report that the assistant “got slow this week”. The timeline shows searching unchanged at 220 ms and the courier check up from 180 ms to 2.4 s after the courier's own upgrade. Ten minutes of investigation instead of a day.one question · nine steps · one shared ID linking them all

what happens without it

Nine healthy systems and one slow product, with no way to connect the two. Record the model used, the pieces counted and the pages read, and the same timeline starts answering cost questions too.

Twenty decisions, one question

Read as a glossary, these twenty terms are trivia to memorise. Read as a path, they are a checklist for any AI product going live: what did you decide about splitting the documents, about searching them, about which model answers, about what it remembers, about which systems it may touch, about what it is allowed to believe, and about how anyone would ever know it was right?

Notice where the difficulty actually sits. Only about half of this page is about the AI model at all. The rest — serving several customers at once, giving up on a broken system, saving progress, handing out read-only keys, keeping one joined-up timeline — is ordinary software engineering, applied to a part that is expensive and never gives the same answer twice. That is the half most teams underestimate, and it is the half that decides whether a good demo survives real customers.

The question we followed came back in three seconds, with the right reason, a new delivery date and a refund, and two sources the customer could check. Twenty decisions, and not one of them was a smarter model.
